## 2. Country Analysis

### 2.1. Legal Framework

The UK’s employment law frame work is amongst the most comprehensive in the world, it balances employee protections with business flexibility through a mix of laws, regulations and case law. The legal foundation Employment Rights Act 2025 which received Royal Assent on Dec 18 2025, represents the most significant restructure of UK Employment law in decades and will be implemented in phases from April 2026 onwards (Gov of UK, 2025). The Act also introduces various wide reforms which includes stronger protections against unfair dismissal through a reduced qualifying period, new restrictions on the use of “fire and rehire” practices, and enhanced day-one employment rights (Gov of UK, 2025); (ACAS, 2026). In addition, the restructures strengthen employer responsibilities around workplace dignity by expanding protections against sexual harassment and limiting the enforceability of non-disclosure agreements in cases involving harassment or discrimination (ACAS, 2026). The Act also establishes a Fair Work Agency to consolidate enforcement responsibilities across minimum wage compliance, agency worker standards, and labour market abuses, reinforcing regulatory oversight while maintaining business flexibility (Gov of UK, 2025).

The Equality Act 2010 prohibits discrimination across nine protected characteristics which are age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage/civil partnership, pregnancy/maternity, race, religion/belief, sex, sexual orientation, In addition organizations with 250 or more employees must publish annual gender pay gap data (ACAS, 2026). Other Laws includes the National Minimum Wage act 1998 which requires minimum wage of £12.71/hour for age group 21+ from April 2026 (Gov of UK, 2025), Working Time Regulations 1998 states 48-hour week limit and 5.6 weeks paid holiday (ACAS, 2026), Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 and Trade Union and Labour Relations Act 1992 protects collective bargaining rights (legalisation.gov.uk, 2026).

![Figure 3: Minimum Wage (Gov of UK, 2025)](images/hrm-uk/fig-3.jpg)

Recent regulatory and enforcement developments in the UK have aimed to address weaknesses associated with gig and other non-standard forms of work mainly through clearer understanding of worker status and strengthened oversight of employment practices (Cambridge Centre For Business Research, 2026). While the UK continues to maintain relatively flexible contractual arrangements compared with many OECD countries, these targeted safeguards operate alongside lower overall employment protection levels, reflecting the characteristics of a liberal market economy (Cambridge Centre For Business Research, 2026). For organizations, compliance with these requirements increases the need for strong HR systems but can also enhance employee trust and support workforce retention in highly competitive labour markets (Cambridge Centre For Business Research, 2026).

### 2.2. Cultural Factors

It has been found that the cultural context plays an important role in shaping the HRM practices, especially regarding leadership styles, communication styles, and the employee-employer relationship, in the United Kingdom. From an international HRM perspective the workplace culture of the UK can be analyzed using the well-established cross-cultural theories, such as Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions Theory and the GLOBE leadership theory (Hofstede, 2001) ; (House, et al., 2004).

According to Hofstede’s model, the UK scores highly on individualism and relatively low on power distance, showing a cultural favorism for personal independence, merit-based achievement, and limited hierarchical separation between managers and employees (Hofstede, 2001). These cultural characteristics support HRM practices that emphasizes individual responsibility, performance-based rewards, and employee voice. In practice, this is reflected in flatter organizational structures and an expectation that employees participate actively in discussions, decision-making, and problem solving processes.

![Figure 4: Hofstede model](images/hrm-uk/fig-4.jpg)

Evidences from the GLOBE study further supports this cultural profile. The UK is related with leadership styles that value participative, charismatic, and performance-oriented behaviors, while rejecting strict and status based leadership approaches (House, et al., 2004). Because of this, the leadership efficiency in UK organizations is closely related to communication skills, emotional intelligence and the ability to motivate and engage in teams. From an HRM point, this places importance on leadership development programmes that promote coaching, collaboration, and inclusive leadership capabilities.

Cultural expectations around fairness and equality are strongly incorporated within UK organizations. These standards align with Hofstede’s importance on individual rights and with the GLOBE dimension of human oriented leadership supporting expectations of equal treatment and respect in the workplace (Hofstede, 2001) (House, et al., 2004). As a result, HR policies related to recruitment, promotion, and rewards are expected to demonstrate transparency and fairness, while diversity and inclusion initiatives are viewed as both cultural expectations and organizational priorities.

The workforce within the UK is becoming increasingly multicultural due to the dynamics of talent flow within a globalized world. This reality, therefore, requires that human resource managers move beyond national cultural norms and focus on developing cultural competence and cultural intelligence. Because of this, effective international Human Resource Management within a UK context involves creating a balance between cultural norms and diversity, thereby reinforcing the strategic importance of culturally informed Human Resource Management within a globalized world.

### 2.3. Economic Context

The economic background of the United Kingdom also largely impacts the human resource management practices, especially in relation to the labor supply, skills development, wage systems, and workforce planning. By being a mature and service-based economy, the United Kingdom’s labor market experiences a high level of employment in sectors such as finance, professional services, technology, healthcare, and retail, which continuously creates the demand for human resources. (Office For National Statistics, 2026).

In the recent years the UK economy has seen reasonable growth alongside constant structural challenges. Economic uncertainty due to inflationary pressures, post-pandemic adjustment, and changing global trade dynamics has contributed to labour market tension and rising employment costs. Forecasts from Goldman Sachs Research recommends that UK economic growth could reach around 1.4% in 2026, following slower growth in 2025, however productivity growth is expected to remain relatively weak which increases pressure on organizations to improve workforce efficiency through strategic HRM involvements (Goldman Sachs, 2026). As a result, HR roles are increasingly expected to support organizational resilience through effective workforce planning, performance management, and skills development.

![Figure 5: GDP monthly estimate, UK: November 2025 (Office for National Statistics, 2025)](images/hrm-uk/fig-5.jpg)

A key characteristic of the UK economic context is the majority of skills shortages mainly in sectors such as technology, engineering, healthcare, and construction. Data from the Office for National Statistics and CIPD highlight ongoing gaps in both technical and soft skills which increases the competition for talent and reliance on upskilling, reskilling, and flexible employment arrangements (Office For National Statistics, 2026); (CIPD, 2025). These conditions have raised the strategic importance of learning and development, employer branding, and employee retention initiatives within the UK organizations.

![Figure 6: Industries in the UK (House of Commons Library, 2024)](images/hrm-uk/fig-6.jpg)

In the UK, the wage dynamics have had a significant impact on the implementation of HRM practices. The increasing cost of living and the National Living Wage legislation are placing increasing pressure on pay systems, especially in lower-paid industries. At the same time, employers must balance the need to control costs with the need to appease employees in terms of what they perceive as fair pay and rewards, and a good balance between work and life. As a result, many employers are now using total reward approaches, which involve a combination of financial and non-financial rewards

Overall, the economic situation of the UK supports the strategic role that HRM plays in supporting organizational performance. The limitations that exist within the UK’s labor market require that HR managers use adaptive methods that are data-driven in order to ensure that workforce capabilities are aligned with organizational needs in a developing economic situation.

### 2.4. HR Policies and Practices

The policies and practices of human resource in the United Kingdom are shaped by a mixture of legal constraints, cultural factors, and economic factors. Organizations in the UK are characterized by relatively official HR systems as a result of strict regulatory controls. At the same time, organizations in the UK are able to adapt to changing labour market conditions and competitive forces. Consequently, HRM in UK organizations is becoming more strategic in support of organizational performance rather than being seen as a purely administrative activity (CIPD, 2025).

#### 2.4.1. Recruitment and Selection

Recruitment and selection practices in the UK are strongly influenced by equality, fairness, and transparency principles incorporated in employment legislation and organizational standards. Employers are expected to apply non-discriminatory recruitment processes using clear job descriptions, competency based interviews, and unbiased selection criteria (ACAS, n.d.). In response to skills shortages and tighter labour markets, many organizations have started to use their digital recruitment platforms, online assessments, and data driven hiring tools. CIPD research shows that employer branding has become increasingly important in attracting skilled candidates, mainly in sectors experiencing constant talent shortages (CIPD, 2025).

#### 2.4.2. Training and Development

Training and development is a key area of HRM in the UK, which is being continuously driven by skills gaps, technological shifts, and issues of productivity. Organizations are now investing in continuous learning, re-skilling, and development initiatives for their workforce to enhance their flexibility and performance over time (Office For National Statistics, 2026) (CIPD, 2025). Leadership development programmes are being prioritized for developing ‘soft skills’ in leaders and managers, such as communication skills, emotional intelligence, and inclusive leadership, in line with cultural requirements as well as managing diverse and hybrid workforces (CIPD, 2025).

#### 2.4.3. Performance Management

Performance management practices in the UK have moved away from the traditional annual-based performance evaluation practices towards more developmental practices like performance checks, ongoing feedback, coaching-based management, and flexible goal-setting systems. This is because these practices are more focused on the development, engagement, and skills progression of the employees, as opposed to performance evaluation only (CIPD, 2025). This is also in line with the cultural values of involving employees in the decision-making process, as well as the performance benefits of ongoing performance dialogue.

#### 2.4.4. Rewards and Employee Relations

Rewards system in the UK is influenced by legal requirements for wages, increasing living costs, and what is expected of employees in terms of balance between work and life. The recent increase in the national living wage (£12.71/hour for 21+ from April 2026) has put pressure on the pay systems, especially for lower-paid jobs, hence forcing organizations to consider their reward systems (Gov of UK, n.d.). It is because of this that many employers have started to offer total reward approaches, where financial and non-financial rewards, such as flexible working arrangements, well-being initiatives, and development opportunities, are provided to employees (CIPD, 2025). Employee relations have been supported through formal consultation, grievance arrangements, and in some cases, collective bargaining, supporting trust and compliance within the employment relationship (ACAS, 2026).

Overall, HR policies and practices in the UK reflects a balance between regulatory requirements, cultural values, and business needs. This approach has helped organizations in the UK to attract, develop, and retain their talent pool while dealing with economic uncertainty and competitive labor markets.

### 2.5. Diversity and Inclusion

Diversity and inclusion (D&I) are key component of HRM in the United Kingdom influenced by legal obligations, cultural expectations, and organizational strategies aimed to improve fairness and performance. International migration, demographic change, and growing engagement of underrepresented groups in the labour market have all contributed to the UK workforce's diversity. Due to this, Diversity & Inclusion is both a compliance need and a strategic HR focus.

From a legal point of view, as discussed in Section 2.1 (Legal Framework), the Equality Act 2010 provides the foundation for workplace diversity and inclusion in the UK by prohibiting discrimination across nine protected characteristics, which includes age, gender, race, disability, religion, and sexual orientation. This legislation requires organizations to ensure fairness in recruitment, promotion, pay, and dismissal processes and has driven the widespread adoption of formal equality, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) policies across UK organizations (ACAS, n.d.). Compliance with equality legislation are important for HR managers because it not only reduces legal risk but also promotes ethical employment practices and inclusive workplace environments.

Beyond compliance, many UK organizations increasingly recognize the business case for diversity and inclusion. The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development (CIPD) found that inclusive workplaces are related with better levels of employee engagement, innovation, and retention, particularly in knowledge-intensive and service based industries (CIPD, 2024). As a result, HR practices are increasingly focusing on inclusive recruitment, unconscious bias training, diverse leadership pipelines, and flexible working arrangements to serve employees with varying demands and life circumstances.

The role of leadership and organizational culture cannot be underestimated in the implementation of the D&I policy. Fairness, openness, and respect are all significant qualities of inclusive leadership that are crucial in creating a psychologically safe environment, making all employees feel valued and enabled to perform. The role of HR operations is significant in promoting inclusion through various initiatives.

However, there are various challenges in achieving this objective. Issues like gender and ethnicity pay gaps, underrepresentation in leadership positions, and difficulties in accommodating and managing a workforce of the disabled show that having formal D&I strategies may not be enough. This paved the way for the increasing focus on data-driven D&I strategies like workforce analytics and pay gap reporting, etc.

![Figure 7: Gender Pay gaps (Office for National Statistics, 2024)](images/hrm-uk/fig-7.jpg)

In conclusion, diversity and inclusion in the UK are a blend of legislative, cultural, and opportunity aspects. The success of a good Diversity and Inclusion strategy enables organizations to access a larger talent pool, improve employee engagement, and sustain organizational success in a diverse and competitive market.

UK HRM shows a unique balance between strong labor rights and company flexibility, placing it between coordinated market countries (Germany, Nordics) and liberal market economies (US, Australia).

### 3.2. Cultural Insights and Leadership practices

Another set of differences that influence HR practices in different nations are cultural differences. According to Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, UK has a very individualistic and low power distance society, which aligns with the concept of participative leadership. When compared with the US, it reflects similar individualistic tendencies but with a need for direct feedback and pressure on performance. Similarly, with respect to Germany, it reflects higher uncertainty avoidance with formal consultation. In contrast, Japan and China reflect higher power distance and collectivistic societies, which align with hierarchical leadership.

Another set of differences that can impact the HR practices in various countries is the cultural differences. ## 4. Recommendations

### Problem 1: Persistent Skills Shortages and Weak Workforce Planning

Despite a developed HR infrastructure, UK organizations still experiences skills shortages in various sectors including but not limited to technology, healthcare, and engineering. Current workforce planning is often reactive, relying heavily on external recruitment and international labour markets rather than long-term talent development.

**Recommendation:** The organizations in the UK should improve strategic workforce planning by incorporating labour market data, business forecasting, and skills audits into HR decision-making. Increased investment in reskilling, upskilling, and apprenticeship programmes can support long-term talent sustainability and reduce reliance on external labour supply, mainly in international operations.

### Problem 2: Managing Cultural Diversity and Inclusive Leadership

Multicultural workforce is growing rapidly in the UK, mainly in multinational organizations. However, most managers often lack cultural importance and inclusive leadership capability, which then leads to communication barriers, disengagement, and underutilization of wider talents.

**Recommendation:** HR functions should include inclusive leadership development into management training, mainly with a focus on cultural importance, inclusive decision-making, and people centered leadership behaviors. This would improve collaboration in cross cultural teams and boost employee engagement and retention.

### Problem 3: Compliance-Driven Diversity and Inclusion Practices

While the organizations in the UK, generally comply with equality legislation, diversity and inclusion initiatives are often treated as compliance exercises rather than strategic priorities which limits their impact on organizational performance.

**Recommendation:** Organizations should start using data driven Diversity and Inclusion strategies, using workforce analytics, pay gap analysis, and employee feedback methods to identify structural inequalities. By linking D&I outcomes to leadership accountability would improve implementation and move practice beyond minimum legal compliance.

### Problem 4: Traditional Performance and Reward Systems

Although several progress has been made, still some UK organizations rely on rigid performance appraisal systems and pay structures that fail to reflect changing employee expectations around development, wellbeing, and work life balance.

**Recommendation:** UK employers should continue transitioning towards development focused performance management supported by regular feedback and coaching. The reward systems should be reviewed regularly to adopt total reward approaches while combining pay with flexible working, wellbeing support, and career development opportunities.

### Problem 5: Inconsistent HR Practices in International Operations

UK-based multinational organizations often struggle to balance global HR consistency with local legal and cultural requirements, which leads to governance gaps and employee dissatisfaction.

**Recommendation:** HR leaders should improve global HR governance frameworks, establish clear core policies while allowing structured local adaptation. This would ensure that there are ethical employment practices, regulatory compliance, and consistency across international operations.

### Overall Impact

Addressing these challenges through targeted, strategic HR involvements would improve the sustainability and effectiveness of HRM in the UK. By moving from reactive and compliance focused practices to proactive, inclusive, and data driven HRM, the organizations in the UK can improve employee performance and competitiveness in international contexts.

## 5. Conclusion

The following report undertook an analysis of human resource management in the United Kingdom in relation to international human resource management, focusing on the legal, cultural, economic, and organizational dimensions of HRM in relation to people management in the UK. According to this report, the UK's HRM environment is characterized by a balance of regulatory protection and labor market flexibility in terms of a strong legal environment, participative cultural context, and well- developed HR infrastructures.

It is clear that although organizations in the UK are able to benefit from a strong employment environment in terms of employment laws, appropriate HR practices, and a strategic performance management system, there are still many challenges that remain. Additionally, in terms of diversity and inclusion policies, these are well-developed in the UK but often require stronger implementation and accountability in terms of achieving desired results.

Moreover, in terms of comparing the UK's HRM environment in relation to international HRM, it is clear that the UK represents a middle position between liberal and coordinated market economies in terms of providing stronger employment regulations than flexible economies while remaining more flexible than highly regulated economies.

This would be important for organizations that operate in multiple countries. Overall, it is clear that for there to be sustainable and appropriate HRM in the UK, there is a need for organizations in this country to be able to effectively transition from compliance-based HRM practices to more proactive, inclusive, and data-driven people management.
